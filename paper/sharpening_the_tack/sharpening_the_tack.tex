\begin{abstract}
The fundamental challenge of computational biogerontology is understanding whether biological aging can be mathematically detected and computationally reversed using continuous-time State Space Models. In this study, we investigate the thermodynamic properties of organismal trajectories to address this core question. We hypothesize that aging manifests as a measurable geometric flattening of the macroscopic Waddington basin, corresponding to a critical loss of system resilience and stability. Consequently, we propose that injecting exogenous precision will artificially steepen this basin, thereby rescuing failing biological limit cycles. By formally modeling the energy landscape of these biological systems, this framework provides a rigorous mathematical mechanism to quantify age-related pathological decline. Ultimately, sharpening the thermodynamic basin presents a novel computational paradigm for both detecting complex pathological states and simulating targeted, physics-informed interventions in aging biological systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Methods}
The simulation environment models a multiscale physics engine defined over a continuous 46-dimensional global state space. This state space is rigorously partitioned into two hierarchical observer scales---a Micro observer and a Macro observer---structured via Fristonian Markov Blankets. Formally, the state space of each observer is decomposed into conditionally independent sets: internal, sensory, active, and external states. 

The Micro observer isolates a 30-dimensional local subspace, comprising 8 internal states, 6 sensory states, 8 active states, and 8 external states. In parallel, the Macro observer isolates a 16-dimensional local subspace, formulated with 4 internal states, 4 sensory states, 4 active states, and 4 external states.

The intrinsic dynamics of these nested blankets are governed by a learned energy landscape, which is parameterized by a specialized neural network architecture denoted as the PrecisionWeightedEBM. To guarantee that the learned physical dynamics remain smooth and twice-differentiable---an essential condition for accurately computing continuous forces and gradients---the architecture relies on a shared Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) backbone employing Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) activations. The capacity of the MLP backbone is scaled according to the observer level: the Micro observer's EBM is constructed with a depth of 2 and a hidden dimension of 32, whereas the Macro observer's EBM utilizes a depth of 2 and a hidden dimension of 16. Both models operate at a fixed thermodynamic temperature parameter of 1.00.

Following the shared backbone, the network bifurcates into two distinct output heads to characterize the state-dependent free energy. The first is a scalar Energy head, which approximates the local scalar potential. The second is a Precision head, which projects the network activations to construct a state-dependent precision matrix. To ensure numerical stability and valid probabilistic interpretations, the precision matrix must be strictly Symmetric Positive-Definite (SPD). The Precision head enforces this constraint algebraically via a Cholesky decomposition, outputting the non-zero elements of a lower triangular matrix $L$ and computing the precision matrix as $L L^{\top}$.

Temporal dependencies in the dataset are captured using a sequence length of 10. During inference, the physics engine propagates continuous-time trajectories using a numerical integration scheme with a discrete time-step of $dt = 0.0100$. The full simulation protocol executes 5 independent runs, with each run simulating a trajectory of 3000 integration steps. To quantify structural stability and resilience, the engine applies parameterized interventions configured strictly at $\lambda_A = 0.200$ and $\lambda_B = 5.00$. To ensure strict computational reproducibility across all dynamics and network initializations, the global pseudo-random number generator seed is fixed to 42.

\section{Results}
To establish the baseline biological dynamics of aging in \textit{C. elegans}, we quantified systemic differences between young and old cohorts across the time domain, spectral domain, and thermodynamic entropy production. 

In the time domain, we evaluated the manifestation of critical slowing down to assess dynamic stability. The analysis revealed a mean critical slowing down metric of 0.925 in young worms, compared to a markedly reduced value of 0.117 in old worms. The mechanical resilience of these states can be mathematically conceptualized using the Hessian trace of the system's potential landscape. Within this analytical framework, it is crucial to note that a higher Hessian trace is better; it indicates a steep, robust thermodynamic basin that effectively resists stochastic noise. Conversely, a low trace indicates a flattened, pathological landscape where restoring forces are compromised, which is characteristic of the degraded state observed in the older organisms.

Despite the pronounced differences in time-domain stability, spectral domain characteristics remained highly conserved across the lifespan. The peak frequency was calculated at 0.00267 for the young \textit{C. elegans} cohort and maintained an identical value of 0.00267 in the old cohort. This exact parity suggests that the fundamental oscillatory periodicity of the biological system remains structurally intact even as the overall basin of attraction flattens.

Finally, we examined the non-equilibrium thermodynamic state of the organisms via entropy production. The young cohort exhibited an entropy production value of 5.47. In stark contrast, the old cohort demonstrated a severely attenuated entropy production of 0.0674. This substantial reduction further corroborates the time-domain findings, illustrating a clear transition from a highly active, robust biological state to a flattened, energy-depleted thermodynamic landscape in aged specimens.

The baseline Gaussian model yielded a KS-statistic of 0. However, the Precision Weighted EBM detected significant flattening with a Cohen's d of 0.283.

Our analysis reveals a stark contrast in the capacity of the evaluated models to detect distributional shifts associated with aging. The standard Gaussian model entirely failed to differentiate between age cohorts, yielding identical predictive distributions for both young ($\mu = 117$, $\sigma = 0$) and old ($\mu = 117$, $\sigma = 0$) subjects. Consequently, the Gaussian model produced a Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) statistic of $0$ ($p = 1.00$) and an undefined Cohen's $d$ (nan), indicating no measurable distributional divergence. In contrast, the PrecisionWeightedEBM successfully captured the aging signal by extracting distinct population parameters for the young ($\mu = 61.5$, $\sigma = 32.6$) and old ($\mu = 53.6$, $\sigma = 22.7$) cohorts. This parameter resolution enabled the PrecisionWeightedEBM to achieve a statistically significant distributional separation, specifically evidenced by a KS-statistic of $0.119$ ($p = 0$) and a quantifiable effect size yielding a Cohen's $d$ of $0.283$, thereby demonstrating its superior empirical sensitivity to age-related biological variations.

The thermodynamic rescue of the system was evaluated by shifting the intervention parameter lambda from 0.200 to 5.00 over a 3000-step period with a temporal resolution of 0.0100. This modification induced a massive spike in the Hessian trace, which escalated from a baseline overall mean of 19.2 with a standard deviation of 0.923 to an overall mean of 449 with a standard deviation of 18.7 during the rescue phase. The magnitude of this structural shift completely separated the pre-intervention and post-intervention distributions, yielding a Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic of 1.00 with a p-value of 0 and a Wasserstein distance of 430. The overwhelming scale of this topological transition is ultimately characterized by the intervention's Cohen's d effect size of -32.4, quantitatively confirming a highly substantial and unambiguous thermodynamic rescue of the system's dynamics.
