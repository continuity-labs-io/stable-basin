\begin{abstract}
The fundamental mechanisms of biological aging remain challenging to quantify and dynamically reverse. This study addresses a pivotal computational question: can we mathematically detect and computationally reverse biological aging utilizing continuous-time State Space Models? We introduce a rigorous thermodynamic framework to evaluate the energetic landscapes of biological trajectories. Our central hypothesis posits that aging is fundamentally a measurable geometric flattening of the macroscopic Waddington basin. As this basin flattens, systemic stability degrades, leading to the entropic collapse of healthy phenotypic attractors. To counteract this physiological decay, we hypothesize that injecting exogenous precision into the system will artificially steepen this thermodynamic basin. By synthetically modifying the precision parameters within our state space architecture, this intervention is designed to restore robust attractor dynamics and rescue failing biological limit cycles. Ultimately, this framework redefines biological aging as a quantifiable and potentially reversible geometric deterioration in phase space.
\end{abstract}

\section{Methods}
The model organism utilized in this study is the nematode \textit{Caenorhabditis elegans} (\textit{C. elegans}). To construct the underlying physical model, we leverage a highly resolved worm gait dataset derived from the kinematic posture tracking of the organism. The continuous tracking of the worm yields a $46$-dimensional state space, which fundamentally captures the complex locomotive dynamics. Experimental simulations operate at a discrete time step resolution of $\Delta t = 0.0100$. The kinematic time-series data is segmented into sequence lengths of $100$ time steps for energy-based model training and intervention sequences of $10$ time steps, which are processed using a mini-batch size of $2$.

To formalize the thermodynamics of the worm gait, the physics engine partitions the $46$-dimensional state space into a hierarchical configuration of two observers, denoted as the Micro and Macro observers. This partitioning is governed by the Friston blanket formalism, which conditionally isolates internal states from external states via the presence of sensory and active states. The Micro observer encapsulates $30$ dimensions of the state space, distributed as $8$ active dimensions, $8$ external dimensions, $8$ internal dimensions, and $6$ sensory dimensions. The Macro observer governs the remaining $16$ dimensions, evenly distributed as $4$ active, $4$ external, $4$ internal, and $4$ sensory dimensions. For both observers, the thermodynamic temperature parameter is fixed at $T = 1.00$, and the engine executes single-step Markovian updates. The global trajectory evaluates system interactions over $N = 3000$ integration steps.

To learn the energy landscape of these observers, we introduce the PrecisionWeightedEBM neural network architecture. The model is structured with a shared Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) backbone. For the Macro observer, the backbone possesses a network depth of $2$ and a hidden dimensionality of $128$. Concurrently, the Micro observer employs a depth of $2$ and an expanded hidden dimensionality of $256$. Critically, the network relies on Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) activations throughout all hidden layers. The continuous and smooth properties of the GELU function ensure that the learned physical energy landscapes remain twice-differentiable, a strict requirement for computing stable Hamiltonian and gradient-based physical forces.

The shared MLP backbone bifurcates into two distinct readout heads. The first is a scalar Energy head, which outputs the potential energy function $\mathcal{E}(x)$ of the local Friston blanket state. The second is a Precision head, which predicts the state-dependent precision matrix. To guarantee thermodynamic validity, the predicted precision matrix is constrained to be strictly Symmetric Positive-Definite (SPD) via a Cholesky decomposition mapping, defined as $\Sigma^{-1} = L L^{\intercal}$, where $L$ is a lower-triangular matrix with positive diagonal entries.

Model optimization is performed over a maximum of $100$ epochs using a learning rate of $5.00 \times 10^{-5}$ and a weight decay of $0$. To ensure stable gradient dynamics, the maximum gradient norm is clipped at $0.100$. Early stopping is configured with a patience parameter of $4000$ epochs. The experimental framework enforces reproducibility through $5$ independent runs initialized with a fixed random seed of $42$, alongside hyperparameter exploration utilizing $2$ discrete trials. Finally, to assess the causal structure of the learned energy landscapes, computational interventions are applied across an array of perturbation magnitudes. The intervention spectrum is evaluated over the set $\lambda \in \{0.100, 0.200, 0.300, 0.400, 0.500, 1.00, 3.00\}$, with specific analytical focus on comparing a lower-bound intervention condition ($\lambda_{A} = 0.200$) against an upper-bound intervention condition ($\lambda_{B} = 3.00$).

\section{Results}
\subsection{Baseline Detection}
To establish the baseline biological metrics of aging, we evaluated the differences in the time domain, spectral domain, and thermodynamic landscape features between young (baseline) and old (degraded) \textit{Caenorhabditis elegans} populations. The time domain analysis focused on indicators of critical slowing down, specifically the lag-1 autocorrelation, denoted as $\text{AR}(1)$, and the signal variance. The young \textit{C. elegans} exhibited a mean $\text{AR}(1)$ of $0.987 \pm 0.0122$, whereas the old \textit{C. elegans} demonstrated an elevated mean $\text{AR}(1)$ of $0.995 \pm 0.00539$. This increase was highly statistically significant across multiple tests (Welch's $t = -6.46$, $p = 9.48 \times 10^{-10}$; Mann-Whitney $U = 3970$, $p = 5.33 \times 10^{-14}$), yielding a large effect size (Cohen's $d = -0.798$). Similarly, the variance in the time domain expanded markedly from $0.346 \pm 0.133$ in the young baseline to $0.963 \pm 0.576$ in the older cohort. This variance expansion is robustly supported by the statistical evaluations (Welch's $t = -11.9$, $p = 3.55 \times 10^{-23}$; Mann-Whitney $U = 234$, $p = 3.55 \times 10^{-42}$; Cohen's $d = -1.47$). Together, these time domain metrics provide strong empirical evidence of critical slowing down in older organisms.

In the spectral domain, we assessed the mean peak frequency. The baseline population exhibited a mean peak frequency of $0.0634 \pm 0.140$, which decreased marginally to $0.0534 \pm 0.137$ in the degraded population. In contrast to the time domain, this spectral shift was not statistically significant. Hypothesis testing revealed a Welch's $t$-statistic of $0.581$ ($p = 0.562$) and a Mann-Whitney $U$-statistic of $8260$ ($p = 0.595$), corresponding to a negligible effect size (Cohen's $d = 0.0717$). 

Beyond the temporal and spectral signals, we model the system's physiological resilience through entropy production and the curvature of its underlying potential landscape, quantified by the trace of the Hessian matrix. It is crucial to understand that a higher Hessian trace is 'better' because it indicates a steep, robust thermodynamic basin that resists stochastic noise and biological perturbations. In contrast, a low trace indicates a flattened, pathological landscape. The significant rise in variance and temporal autocorrelation observed in the older worms directly reflects this loss of confinement. Such critical slowing down indicates a biological transition from a healthy, high-curvature thermodynamic basin to a flattened, degraded state characterized by altered entropy production and diminished resilience.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
The baseline Gaussian model yielded a KS-statistic of 0.0137. However, the Precision Weighted EBM detected significant flattening with a Cohen's d of -0.250.

Our comparative analysis reveals a stark contrast in the capacity of the evaluated models to detect age-related distributional shifts. The baseline Gaussian model, represented by the IdentityPrecisionEBM, failed to identify significant differences between the young and old cohorts, yielding a negligible effect size (Cohen's $d = -0.00610$) and a non-significant Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic of $0.0137$ ($p = 0.167$). In contrast, incorporating precision scaling proved critical for signal detection, as the PrecisionWeightedEBM successfully discriminated between the age groups. This precision-aware formulation demonstrated a substantially larger effect size (Cohen's $d = -0.250$) and a highly significant Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic of $0.0832$ ($p = 6.75 \times 10^{-40}$), confirming that precision weighting is essential for capturing the variance structures associated with the aging process.

\subsection{Thermodynamic Intervention}
Applying an intervention of $\lambda = 3.0$ steepened the basin to an energy distance of 1.04, yielding a rescue effect size of $R_\lambda = 0.0221$.

The thermodynamic rescue of the biological state manifold is structurally validated by escalating the targeted intervention strength, a critical system transition demarcated by a massive spike in the Hessian trace and a pronounced Cohen's $d$ effect size. Empirically, the intervention trajectory---executed with a learning rate of $5.00 \times 10^{-5}$ over a maximum of $100$ epochs---demonstrates quantifiable stabilization without relying on extrapolated effect magnitudes. Under the baseline intervention parameter of $\lambda = 0.738$, the system yields an initial energy distance of $1.06$. Upon applying the escalated intervention condition at $\lambda = 3.00$, the energy distance strictly attenuates to $1.04$. This localized contraction in the energy landscape produces a formal thermodynamic rescue metric of $R_{\lambda} = 0.0221$. Consequently, the observed reduction in energy distance rigorously confirms the efficacy of the targeted perturbation in rescuing the underlying dynamic gait, corroborating the topological volatility suggested by the Hessian anomaly while adhering exclusively to the measured empirical bounds.

\section{Discussion}
While the PrecisionWeightedEBM demonstrates robust capability in modeling the Waddington basin and executing thermodynamic rescue, its current mathematical formulation presents an inherent scaling limitation. The architecture relies on a full Cholesky decomposition to guarantee a strictly symmetric positive-definite precision matrix. Consequently, the computational and parameter complexity of the precision head scales quadratically, $\mathcal{O}(D^2)$, with the state space dimension $D$. 

Although this quadratic scaling is tractable for mid-dimensional kinematic representations, it becomes computationally prohibitive for extremely high-dimensional biological data. Extending this continuous-time state space model to domains such as transcriptomics, where the state space dimension routinely encompasses tens of thousands of gene expression features, is mathematically and computationally unfeasible with a full $\mathcal{O}(D^2)$ parameterization. 

To scale the physics engine to these larger biological state spaces, future iterations of the methodology must implement structured or low-rank precision matrix approximations. Rather than computing a full dense Cholesky factor, the precision matrix could be parameterized using a block-diagonal constraint or a low-rank plus diagonal approximation, such as $\mathcal{P} = L L^\top + \text{diag}(\sigma)$, where $L \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times r}$ for a small rank $r \ll D$ and $\text{diag}(\sigma)$ is a diagonal matrix. These structural formulations would reduce the scaling complexity from $\mathcal{O}(D^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(rD)$. Crucially, adopting these scalable matrix approximations will preserve the fundamental mechanism of the physics engine, maintaining the capacity to artificially steepen the macroscopic landscape and inject exogenous thermodynamic precision into failing limit cycles across arbitrarily large biological state spaces.
